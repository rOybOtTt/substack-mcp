# 03 — Schabacker, *Stock Market Theory and Practice* (1930)

## 1. Bibliographic info and coverage

- **Author:** R. W. (Richard W.) Schabacker. The text says he built his 1872–1930 stock index while on the financial staff of the Reports Department of the Federal Reserve Bank of New York.
- **Title / publisher / year:** *Stock Market Theory and Practice*. B. C. Forbes Publishing Company, New York, **1930**. The preface is signed "New York City, March 6, 1930". It is US public domain as a 1930 publication.
- **Source used:** archive.org item `bwb_Y0-EGF-847` (metadata: date 1930, creator R. W. Schabacker, publisher B. C. Forbes). The full text is `bwb_Y0-EGF-847_djvu.txt`.
- **Why this book and not the fallbacks:** the 1930 book exists in full text, so I did not use Wyckoff's 1922/1924 or 1919 titles. (Background note from my own knowledge, not from this text: his pattern-heavy *Technical Analysis and Stock Market Profits* is a later book, from 1932, outside the ≤ 1930 window.) This 1930 volume is the earlier, shorter statement of the same chart system ("Seven Cardinal Chart Forecasting Formations").
- **Size:** about 315,000 OCR words in total. About 242,000 words are the 27 chapters; the rest is appendices (member-firm address lists and similar). Chart-label OCR noise is substantial.
- **Coverage (stated honestly):**
  - **Read in full (about 89,000 words):** Ch XIX What Makes the Market Move; XX An Introduction to Trading; XXI Principles of Trading; XXII Taking the Worry Out of Stock Trading; XXIII The Technical Side of the Market; XXIV An Introduction to Chart Reading; XXV Principles of Chart Trading; XXVI Long-Swing Movements; XXVII Conclusion and Review. Also read: the price-earnings section of Ch XVII.
  - **Covered through the detailed chapter synopses plus a keyword scan only, not read line by line:** Ch I–XVIII. These are exchange mechanics, the clearing corporation, margin, short-sale and odd-lot mechanics, brokers' loans, broker statements, outside exchanges, security types, dividends and rights, balance-sheet analysis, and information sources. The scan found no chart or trading rules in them beyond cross-references to Ch XX–XXV.
  - **Skipped as boilerplate:** Appendixes A–L (firm lists, chronology, index constituents).

## 2. Core beliefs (chapter refs; short quotes)

- **Technical factors rule the short term; fundamentals rule the long term.** For the trader, "the technical position of any stock is of utmost importance … even of more importance, quite often, than the fundamental position" (XXI). Technical factors "act much more swiftly than the fundamentals" (XXIV).
- **Nothing is infallible.** "No definite rules can be formulated which are infallible" (XXIII). "Charts are not to be used alone. Charts are not to be trusted implicitly" (XXIV). He is still an "ardent believer in the efficacy of charts" (XXIV).
- **Price action is the footprint of organized money.** Pools run four stages: "(1) Accumulation; (2) Marking Up; (3) Distribution; and (4) Marking Down" (XXIII). Chart formations are how an outsider detects which stage a stock is in (XXIV). He guesses that up to 75% of active issues in a bull market have some sponsorship (XXIV).
- **Supply overhangs old buying zones.** Buyers trapped at a level sell when price returns to it, which creates resistance. Shorts and would-be buyers who missed a low create support at that low (XXIII).
- **The market discounts the news.** There is a "tendency for a stock to react, instead of advance, when a piece of good news comes out" (XXI). The market also discounts business turns months ahead (XIX, XXVI).
- **Crowd psychology drives moves too far.** Public psychology "may therefore be counted upon to carry any major movement far beyond the limits of its logical extent" (XXVII). This is safe to ride early and dangerous late (XIX).
- **Time builds power.** "The longer the picture takes to develop, the more important the succeeding move" (XXV).
- **Volume and the close carry information.** Charts should show the daily high–low, the close and the volume. The open is unimportant (XXIV).

## 3. Explicit decision rules

### 3a. Signals that EXIST (as stated)

**Reversal ("accumulation/distribution") formations, Ch XXIV.** He ranks them by reliability:
1. *Head-and-shoulders* top/bottom and *triangle/coil* are "the most common". When the head or right shoulder misses the "perfect" level in the direction of the expected move, that signals extra force: the Westinghouse 1928 bottom was "stronger for its size", and the NY Central 1928 top had a right shoulder below the left, showing "greater than normal weakness".
2. *Common (rounded) turn*.
3. *Ascending bottom / descending top*: a flat side plus a sloping side. "The direction of the one sloping side indicates the direction of the major movement" before the breakout happens (GM 1928: 135→200 "inside of a single month").
4. *Double top/bottom*: "not quite so certain or definite". It "does not have as much weight, in the author's opinion, as it usually receives credit for". A short double top was "too short and too intermediate to be a strong one" (General Asphalt: −20 points after the main double top, only −6 after the short one).
5. *Triple/quadruple tops*: "successive tops have merely been broken through on the up-side, instead of indicating reaction, almost as often as they have truly indicated distribution".
6. *Complex* (double-head H&S): rare but strong.
7. *Broadening*: five reversals, each wider than the last. Rare, so "comparatively unimportant", though it is "almost a certain signal" when present. Three tops means down.
- **Confluence strengthens a forecast.** When several formations agree, the forecast is stronger (section "How the Chart Approves its Own Forecast", XXIV).

**Triangles/coils, Ch XXIV–XXV.** A narrowing range, often with "decline in volume", shows the floating supply being absorbed. While the triangle forms it does not give the direction. "In most cases the direction in which the stock first breaks from its narrowing range, or pivot, determines the direction of the major movement" (XXIV). "The longer duration of time consumed in formation of the triangle, the larger will be the next big major movement" (XXV). Small *intermediate* triangles are usually **continuation** patterns; they "very seldom reverse the previous movement" (XXV). An ascending triangle in an uptrend means a sharper advance. A descending triangle in a downtrend means a sharper decline, and is "much weaker" than a symmetrical one. Either one appearing against the trend suggests reversal.

**Line/coil after dormancy, Ch XXIII.** Take a stock with past wide swings that "lapsed gradually into quietude for perhaps a year or two" and whose range "will grow gradually narrower". The next major move goes in "the direction of the first large movement away from this gradually narrowing trading range", "barring false moves".

**Continuation micro-patterns, Ch XXV.** The *ascending peak* is 3–5 days in which each day's high and low exceed the prior day's. It is "a particularly strong signal", especially after a one- or two-day "sharp and broad run-up". The *descending bottom* is the mirror image and an "exceptionally weak signal".

**Support/resistance, Ch XXI–XXV.** A level is "a comparatively narrow price range, anywhere from one to 10 points". Levels "generally … are not broken". When one is broken by a "material move", price continues "further and more rapidly in the new direction" (XXV). Longer-formed levels are stronger. A level that was "short, rather quickly established" is "therefore not so likely to hold" (Cerro de Pasco, XXV). A broken support later acts as resistance (the ~230 level in Nov 1929, XXVI). Resistance levels formed on recoveries inside a bear market "are not nearly so dependable" (XXI).

**Retracement rule, Ch XXIII.** A reversal is "normal when it goes about one-third to one-half of the distance of the previous major movement". Less than ⅓ is stronger than normal. More than ½ makes "a more lasting reversal" likely. Example: Radio 1928 rose 85→225 (+140), reacted to just under the 50% level (about 150), then went on toward 300. *Secondary reaction*: the recovery from support should be about half the decline. The secondary reaction should go "only about one-half the distance of the recovery". A secondary reaction that breaks below the primary low is weaker (XXIII).

**End-of-move signals.**
- "One of the best indications of the end of any movement is its reversal during the day on a comparatively large volume of trading" (XXIII).
- A turn is signaled by "growing irregularity, checking of the previous … movement, by heavy trading and perhaps by several 'false starts'" (XXIII).
- *Growing irregularity on high volume* after a straight advance, with quick reversals, shake-outs and failed new highs on heavy volume, means distribution. "Growing irregularity on high volume of transactions almost always indicates weakening technical position, distribution and at least a good-sized intermediate reaction" (XXVI). It means a full reversal only when a clear top pattern (for example, H&S) also forms (XXV, General Railway Signal 1927).
- *Continuation vs exhaustion by day type:* a move is likely to continue "if the movement is not too fast, if most of the day's gain is held at the close and if volume of sales for the day is not too large". It is unlikely to continue if the move is "sudden or convulsive", the gain is not held into the close, and volume is large (XXIII).

**Gaps, Ch XXV.** The *common gap* is usually filled ("perhaps the next day"); for scalpers it is "almost infallible". In Du Pont 1927–28, 16 of 17 gaps were filled. The *breakaway gap* comes out of a completed formation (especially at a triangle apex), is "very seldom covered", and is "the final signal to get aboard". The *exhaustion gap* comes after "a long, swift and major movement". It is filled shortly and is "almost a certain indication that the long and major movement is being exhausted". Gaps are "not 100 per cent. reliable, but … among the most accurate and reliable signals".

**Trend channels, Ch XXV.** Parallel lines drawn through extremes. "Buying points are more dependable in an up trend than are selling points". A failure to reach the upper line warns of weakness, and a break of the channel signals a trend change (Radio 1926). He adds a caution: "the importance of such chart trends is generally over-emphasized".

**Market timing and macro, Ch XIX, XXI, XXVI, XXVII.**
- Overnight shock news: "the chances are usually that the market will do the opposite of what the public does". After the Aug 1929 rate hike the market opened up to 15 points lower near the low, then rallied for weeks.
- A strong, well-defined move at the close, especially after an irregular day, presages strength the next morning.
- Rising call/time-money rates, a rising discount rate, and a rising brokers'-loan ratio are bearish, with a lag of 3–18 months (about 6 normally, about 20 in 1928–29). Bond prices turn before stocks by about 6–8 months.
- "The bull market ends with terrifically high volume while the bear market ends generally with very low volume and little trading interest" (XXVII).
- The 1929 panic stopped (about 6 points through) at a support zone (170–190) that had taken 13 months to form, with seven touches (XXVI). Separately, P/E indexes show the panic "was stemmed at around our logical base of 10 times earnings" (XVII).

### 3b. Entries, exits, stops

- Place a stop on every trade, long or short (XX, XXII). The usual long stop is "from three to six points below the purchase price", set after "studying the normal past action". Place stops "at least a point or more above resistance levels and below support points" to survive false moves (XXV).
- **Reward/risk floor:** "a three-point loss or a five-point profit is about the limit of profit on which successful trading may be expected" (XXII). Never accept "a smaller profit than the limit of possible loss" (XXII).
- **Buy at support with a coupled order.** Example: buy at 76 near support at about 75, stop 72, target 86 (risk 4 to make 10) (XXII, Plate 76).
- **Reverse stops at levels.** Buy near support with an order to sell twice the position if support breaks. Short near resistance with an order to buy twice the position if resistance breaks (NY Central: resistance near 166, reverse stop at about 167½–167¾, the OCR is unclear; then +25 points in under 2 months). Resting stops beat mental stops; the stock ran from 168 to 173 on the breakout day (XXV).
- **Progressive (trailing) stop:** buy at 100, stop 95. At 110 move the stop to 108, at 120 to 117, at 130 to 126, keeping it "a few points under the new high". He prefers this to fixed targets (XXII).
- After a confirmed major reversal, the experienced trader may wait for the first intermediate counter-move to switch sides rather than get stopped at the low (XXIII).
- **Long-swing campaigns:** scale in (about 10% of the intended line every 3–10 points down near apparent bottoms) and scale out (10% every 10–50 points up). "Never entirely completed" (XXVII).
- **Pyramiding** is allowed for experienced traders. "Averaging down" is "much overrated". A drop from 100 to 50 is "almost a certain indication that something is wrong somewhere" (XX).

### 3c. When NOT to trade / no signal / chart unreadable

1. **Panic or abnormal conditions:** "by far the majority refused to trade at all during the Stock Market Panic of 1929. Conditions were too hectic and abnormal" (XXII).
2. **Direction unknown:** when a stock shows a major move coming but not its direction, the trader may wait for it to "declare itself". Better, "The amateur trader would do best to let alone a stock in such an indefinite position" because of false starts (XX).
3. **Late, psychological stage of a move:** "it is best for the conservative long-swing investor to cease his operations and watch the show from the sidelines" (XIX). He also warns about being "right too soon" (XIX, XXVII).
4. **Excess public enthusiasm:** "If everyone is talking about the splendid chance … the chances are that it will not do as well as expected". The author discards recommendations when "public interest, enthusiasm and participation are too great" (XXI).
5. **Overnight news:** "refrain from precipitate action when news of any type breaks overnight"; "it is too late to act after the 'news is out'" (XX, XXI).
6. **Quiet markets in a bull trend:** "Never sell a quiet market short", especially after a shake-out, when volume is drying up at support (XXI, XXIII).
7. **Unconfirmed breaks:** a level break "is not definite unless they are broken by a decided or material margin" (XXV). Shake-outs have "no ironclad or adequate defense" (XXV). Counter-trend trades within a channel are less dependable (XXV).
8. **Weak pattern classes:** double, triple and broadening tops, and trend lines, deserve less weight. So do support levels that formed quickly, and resistance levels formed during bear-market recoveries (XXI, XXIV, XXV).
9. **Trader state:** the "trader's vacation" is warranted when anxious; the most active traders take "two weeks to a month off two to four times a year". "A run of bad luck, whether it results in anxiety or not, is often a signal to get out temporarily" (XXII). Don't set profit goals, don't raise size after success, avoid over-enthusiasm (XXII).
10. **Competence gate:** paper-trade honestly for 2–3 months, with orders entered while the market is closed and filled at the next open. Steady profits need you to be "right even only three times out of five". If you are not, "discontinue trading" (XXII).
11. **Averaging down a loser:** generally do not (XX).

## 4. Testable hypotheses on DAILY OHLCV (my operationalizations; parameters chosen to match the text's point-based examples, most of which are on stocks priced about 50–250)

Defaults: `ATR20`; `V50` = 50-day average volume; pivots from a 5-bar fractal or a zig-zag at 2×ATR; entries at the next open; costs about 10 bps per side; compare with a matched baseline.

- **H-SCH-1 — Coil breakout, direction of first break.** Detection: over L ∈ [15, 120] days, fit lines through pivot highs (negative slope) and pivot lows (positive slope). Require end width ≤ 50% of start width, ≥ 2 touches per side, and 10-day average volume at the end ≤ 0.8× the average over the first 10 days. Trigger: close beyond either line by ≥ 0.5×ATR20. Measure: 20/60-day forward return in the break direction, and the correlation of L with move size ("longer triangle → larger move"). Split by trend context: an intermediate triangle should break in the direction of the prior 60-day trend more than 50% of the time.
- **H-SCH-2 — Ascending/descending triangle predicts direction before the break.** Condition: ≥ 3 pivot highs within ±1.5% (flat top) plus ≥ 3 rising pivot lows, or the mirror. Measure: P(first ≥ 1×ATR close outside the pattern is in the hypotenuse direction) vs 50%; forward 20-day return after the break. Also test: is a descending triangle in a downtrend followed by a larger decline than a symmetrical one?
- **H-SCH-3 — Support holds (fade into support) with reward ≥ 2.5× risk.** Support zone: ≥ 2 pivot lows within ±1.5% (or ±0.5×ATR), ≥ 5 days apart. Entry: close within 1% above the zone in an uptrend (close > 200-day MA). Stop: zone low − 1×ATR (the "point or more" beyond the level). Target: 2.5× risk (his 4-vs-10 example). Measure: hit rate, expectancy, and dependence on zone age (< 20 days vs > 60 days) and touch count. Mirror test: short into resistance formed on recoveries inside downtrends, checking whether it is "not nearly so dependable".
- **H-SCH-4 — Material break of a mature level → continuation (reverse-stop logic).** Condition: zone age ≥ 40 days with ≥ 3 touches; close < zone low − 1×ATR (or 3%). Trade: short, or exit longs. Measure: 20/40-day forward return, and the false-break rate (close back inside the zone within 5 days). Mirror for resistance breaks to the upside (NY Central 1928).
- **H-SCH-5 — Shake-out vs genuine break (volume plus close).** Condition: a support or apex undercut where low < zone − 0.5×ATR but close ≥ zone low, with volume ≥ 1.5×V50 ("sudden increase of volume … with the closing price back up"). Expectation: positive 10/20-day drift, better than (a) a break that closes below the zone and (b) an undercut on low volume. This tests his claim that "a false move will show comparatively high volume".
- **H-SCH-6 — Gap taxonomy.** Common gap: inside a range (no 20-day high or low). Breakaway gap: out of a ≥ 20-day consolidation or triangle to a 20/60-day extreme. Exhaustion gap: in the trend's direction after a ≥ 25% move in ≤ 30 days, or after ≥ 7 of the last 10 days closing in that direction. Measure: P(fill within 1/5/20 days) by type, and the 20-day forward return. Prediction: common gaps fill most often, breakaway gaps fill least and continue, exhaustion gaps fill fast and reverse.
- **H-SCH-7 — Retracement thirds.** After a zig-zag swing ≥ 20% (or ≥ 8×ATR), bucket the counter-move by depth (< ⅓, ⅓–½, > ½). Measure: P(price exceeds the swing extreme within 60 days). Prediction: highest for < ⅓, lowest for > ½. Secondary-reaction variant: after a recovery from a low, a secondary dip that holds above the primary low and retraces ≤ 50% of the recovery → positive 40-day drift.
- **H-SCH-8 — Reversal day on volume ends a move.** Condition in an uptrend: a new 20-day high intraday, close in the bottom 25% of the day's range (CLV ≤ −0.5), volume ≥ 2×V50. Mirror for downtrends. Expectation: 10-day forward return against the prior trend, below baseline. Control: a "healthy" day (close in the top 25% of range, gain ≤ 2×ATR, volume ≤ 1.5×V50) should show continuation.
- **H-SCH-9 — Irregularity on high volume after a straight advance.** Condition: a ≥ 20% rise over ≤ 40 days, then a 10–20-day window where average volume ≥ 1.5× the pre-advance V50, ATR20/price ≥ 1.3× its value during the advance, net change ≤ 1×ATR, and ≥ 2 failed new highs (an intraday new high with a close below the prior high). Expectation: elevated probability of a ≥ 10% drawdown within 30 days. A full reversal only if an H&S top also forms.
- **H-SCH-10 — Quiet pullback in a bull market (don't short it; buy it).** Regime: index > 200-day MA. Condition: stock or index down 7–15% from its 60-day high, then 10 days with average volume ≤ 0.7×V50 and closes within 3% of the pullback low. Expectation: positive 20/40-day forward return. Control: the same pullback with volume ≥ 1.2×V50 ("reaction not over").
- **H-SCH-11 — Ascending peak after a thrust.** Condition: a day with range ≥ 2×ATR20, a close in the top quartile, and a new 20-day high, followed by ≥ 3 consecutive days of higher highs and higher lows. Expectation: positive 10-day forward return. Mirror: the descending bottom after a decline → negative drift.
- **H-SCH-12 — Overnight-panic reversal (index level).** Condition: the index opens ≤ −2% (≤ −3σ of its gap distribution) on a market-wide news day. Trade: buy at the open. Measure: open-to-close and 5/20-day returns vs a matched baseline (the Aug 9 1929 analog).
- **H-SCH-13 — Pattern-class reliability ranking.** Use one detector for H&S, triangle, double, triple and broadening patterns, with the same breakout/stop rules (close beyond the pattern boundary by 0.5×ATR; stop on the opposite side). Prediction to test: success rates rank triangle ≈ H&S > double > triple/broadening.
- **H-SCH-14 (needs rates data) — Credit lead.** Rising 3-month rates or rising corporate-bond yields over 6 months → lower 6–18-month index returns. Bond-price peaks lead index peaks by about 6–8 months.

## 5. Caveats the author admits

- No rule is infallible; all have exceptions. "No iron-clad theory to 'beat the stock market' has ever been developed and none ever will be" (XXII). He favors judgment over formula (XXII).
- Charts "can often be very misleading and can result in considerable loss". False security and neglect of fundamentals are "the chief disadvantages, or dangers, of charts" (XXIV).
- False moves and shake-outs are engineered precisely to defeat chart readers, and there is no complete defense (XXV, XXVII).
- His examples are hand-picked and imperfect. He repeatedly notes formations that "did not come down far enough" or "went slightly too far". On the long Dow chart he gives several alternative labels for the same point ("Or …"), which makes them post-hoc readings.
- He concedes that he wrote most of the book before the 1929 panic. He claims his September 1929 forecasts warned of it.
- On the Sloan backtest (long-swing investing at 73% a year vs buy-and-hold at 32%): "it is much easier to calculate what any market commitment should have been after its long-term results are apparent". That is a hindsight warning.
- The cycle theory is not reliable for timing: "no very satisfactory evidence that cycles in the stock market can be depended upon to any very definite degree" (XXVI).
- He uses an arithmetic scale with point-based levels, and argues that pool managers think in points. That is a scale-dependence issue for modern tests.
- Professional trading is stressful and rarely successful. Most readers are better off as long-swing investors (XXVI).

## 6. Verdict for the rulebook author (10 lines)

1. This is the earliest public-domain codification of the pattern catalog later popularized by Edwards & Magee. Treat it as a source of *hypotheses with priors*, not proven rules.
2. His own confidence ranking is useful: triangles/coils, H&S, breakaway/exhaustion gaps and support/resistance first. Double/triple tops and trend lines are explicitly downgraded.
3. Encode the "signal exists" rule as a contraction (coil) followed by the first material close outside it, confirmed by a breakaway gap or volume. Weight by pattern duration.
4. Encode "no signal" as: direction undeclared, a break smaller than a material margin (~1 ATR), a counter-trend level in a bear market, a quickly formed level, panic or abnormal conditions, or the late euphoric stage.
5. Always pair a level trade with a reverse stop beyond the level, and require reward ≥ risk (his floor is 5:3; his example is 10:4).
6. Volume plus close location is his discriminator between a genuine move and a shake-out, and between continuation and exhaustion. Test it explicitly (H-SCH-5, 8, 9).
7. The ⅓–½ retracement rule and the "quiet pullback in a bull market" rule are simple, regime-aware, and high-priority tests (H-SCH-7, 10).
8. His "intermediate triangles are continuation" claim conflicts with naive symmetric-pattern detectors. Condition on the prior trend.
9. Most of his evidence is illustrative charts from a manipulated pre-SEC market. Demand out-of-sample, cost-inclusive confirmation before adopting any rule.
10. Priority tests: H-SCH-1, 4, 5, 6, 7, 10. Treat H-SCH-14 (credit lead) as a macro overlay, not a stock signal.
