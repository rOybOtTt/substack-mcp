# 02 — Wyckoff ("Rollo Tape"), *Studies in Tape Reading* (1910)

## 1. Bibliographic info and coverage

- **Author:** Richard D. Wyckoff, writing as "Rollo Tape".
- **Title / publisher / year:** *Studies in Tape Reading*. The Ticker Publishing Company, New York. The title page reads "Copyright, 1910, by The Ticker Publishing Company". It is a revised collection of a series first printed in *The Ticker* magazine. The worked examples are dated 1907–1909: Jan 4 1909, Feb 1909, March 1909, June 1909, and the "August, 1909, boom". It is US public domain (pre-1931).
- **Source used:** archive.org item `studiesintaperea00wyckrich` (University of California library scan), file `studiesintaperea00wyckrich_djvu.txt`. A second scan (`studiesintapere01wyckgoog`) was downloaded but not needed.
- **Size:** about 40,000 words of OCR text. That figure includes reproduced tape tables, which are mostly long runs of numbers. The prose is roughly 36,000 words.
- **Chapters read (all of them, in full):** I Introductory; II Preliminary Suggestions; III The Stock List Analyzed; IV Stop Orders, Trading Rules, Etc.; V Volumes and Their Significance; VI Market Technique; VII Dull Markets and Their Opportunities; VIII The Use of Charts as Guides and Indicators; IX Daily Trades vs. Long Pull Operations; X Various Examples and Suggestions; XI Obstacles to be Overcome — Possible Profits; XII Closing the Trades — Suggestions for Students; and the appendix "Two Good Days". I skimmed only the numeric tape and figure-chart tables (the OCR garbles them) and the library boilerplate.
- **Important framing:** this is an *intraday* book. The reader watches the ticker, gets about a 1-point stop, and is usually flat by 3 p.m. Every daily-bar test below is **my translation** of the book's logic to daily bars, not the author's own method.

## 2. Core beliefs (with chapter refs)

- **The tape is the only master. Charts, tips and news come second.** Tape reading is "The science of determining from the tape the immediate trend of prices" (Ch I). He lists what it is *not*: tips, news, "chart indications or by other mechanical methods", "buying on dips and selling on bulges" (Ch I).
- **Anticipation beats reaction.** "Money is made in Tape Reading by anticipating what is coming — not by waiting till it happens and going with the crowd" (Ch I). The tape shows news before the news ticker does. Example: the Consolidated Gas Supreme Court decision, where the stock broke before the announcement (Ch IV).
- **Supply and demand show up as volume at a price.** The market price is a "pair of scales". Large lots taken at the offer mean urgent demand; large lots hitting the bid mean pressure (Ch V).
- **Manipulation follows a readable cycle:** "Accumulation, marking up, and distribution" for a bull campaign. For a short campaign the distribution comes first (Ch V). The operator "must continually show his hand" through volume.
- **Judge the market by what it does *not* do.** "Both the market as a whole and individual stocks are to be judged as much by what they do as what they do not do at critical points" (Ch VI). If a stock shrugs off bad news, or barely reacts when a leader is hit, it is in "strong hands".
- **Trend is defined by structure:** "A series of higher tops and bottoms are made in a pronounced up swing and the reverse in a down swing" (Ch IV, Ch VIII).
- **Line of least resistance:** "Prices follow the line of least resistance" (Ch V). "If a stock or the whole market cannot be advanced, the assumption is that it will decline — a market seldom stands still" (Ch IV).
- **The market moves as one body.** Leaders (the "Big Six") move first and minor stocks follow. He warns: "If a stock is being manipulated higher, the movement will seldom be continued unless other stocks follow" (Ch VI).
- **Success is measured in points, net of costs, over many trades.** The goal is a small average net profit per day, the "nimble eighth" (Ch IX, XI). Costs of about 3/8 point per round trip must be cleared (Ch IV).
- **No formula is possible.** "Each indication is to be judged not so much by rule as according to the conditions surrounding it" (Ch V). He never saw two identical tape "pictures" (Ch XII).

## 3. Explicit decision rules

### 3a. When a signal EXISTS

1. **Volume-at-price imbalance.** If the large lots trade on the up side (taken at the offer) and the small lots on the down side, "Steel will probably sell at 50¼ before 49⅞" (Ch V). The reverse means pressure. Volumes "must be valued in proportion to the activity of the market … No set rule can be established" (Ch V).
2. **Breakout through resistance.** "The stronger the resistance the less likelihood of finding further obstacles in the immediate vicinity". "So when we find a stock emerging into a new field it is best to go with it, especially if … it carries the rest of the market along" (Ch V).
3. **Breakout from a dull, narrow range.** Dullness means the forces are in balance, so "when prices are stationary, we know that from this point there will be a pronounced swing in one direction or another" (Ch VII). "One could almost draw lines on the chart … and buy or sell whenever the line is crossed". The reason: "when a stock shakes itself loose from a narrow radius it is clear that the accumulation or distribution or resting spell has been completed" (Ch VII). In Ch X he adds that if a stock in a 3-point range "suddenly takes on new life and activity, bursting through its former bounds, he must go with it".
4. **Sudden large volume out of a dead market.** Large lots taken at the offer, coming out of a dead market, "can mean only one thing" (Reading, March 27 1909, Ch VII). Kansas City Southern drifted, then burst into activity and volume: "get aboard" (Ch VII).
5. **Reading the direction of a dull market before it breaks.** It is weak "When a dull market shows its inability to hold rallies, or when it does not respond to bullish news". It is strong when there is "gradual hardening in prices; when bear raids fail to dislodge considerable quantities of stock; when stocks do not decline upon unfavorable news" (Ch VII).
6. **Strength under pressure.** Reading was hit for 3 points while Union Pacific lost only a fraction (the OCR suggests ¼), which "proved that they were technically strong" (Ch VI). His drawdown table compares how much of each stock's 1907–09 advance was given back in the Feb 1909 break: UP 14.7%, Reading 33.6%, Steel 44.6%. The smallest giveback marks the strongest hands. "Should a stock fail to break on bad news, it means that insiders have anticipated the decline and stand ready to buy" (Ch VI).
7. **Failure to respond = short signal.** After the Feb 1909 steel news, Steel sat in a ¼-point rut while Union rallied, then broke "with a thud": the cue to go short (Ch VI).
8. **Climax / culmination.** "A long advance or decline usually culminates in a wide, quick movement in the leaders". "An exceptionally violent movement, after a protracted sag or rise, usually indicates its culmination" (Ch VI).
9. **Selling exhaustion at a low.** After a 6¾-point break in UP on Dec 21 1908, the turn was marked by "Larger lots are in demand at the offered prices. There is a final drive but very little stock comes out on it" (Ch IX).
10. **Accumulation evidence.** Rock Island pfd traded volume "out of all proportion to the capital stock outstanding and the floating supply" after a 10-point decline. Distribution at such a low level made no sense, so it read as accumulation (Ch VI). "When insiders shake other people out it means that they want the stock themselves" (Ch VII).
11. **Distribution evidence (abnormal volume in a small range).** On Jan 4 1909, over 50,000 shares of Reading traded "within three-quarters of a point" and the stock still could not advance, which warned of a top (Ch IV). In June 1909 "some 80,000 shares within about half a point of 159" gave "unmistakable notice of a coming decline" (Ch X). Rule: "The best way to distinguish the genuine from the fictitious move is to watch out for abnormally large volumes within a small radius" (Ch X).
12. **Healthy trend checklist (after entry).** "The volume must increase and the rest of the market offer some support or at least not oppose it. The reactions must show a smaller volume than the advances". Also, "each upward swing must be of longer duration and reach a new high level, or it will mean that the rise has spent its force" (Ch X). In a bull market, volume swells on advances and fades "like the ocean at ebb tide" on reactions. If a decline comes on an active ticker and large volume, followed by a sluggish, small-lot rally, that is liquidation (Ch V).
13. **Retracement arithmetic.** "A normal rally is from one-half to two-thirds of the decline". "In case the decline is not over, the rally will fall short" (Ch IX). In a bull market, "a drop of 10 points [is] followed by a recovery of at least half the decline". "If a stock or the market refuses to rally naturally, he knows that the trouble has not been overcome" (Ch VI).
14. **Distribution under cover of a leader / group rotation.** In fall 1907 there was heavy selling in Reading, St. Paul, Copper, Steel and Smelters "under cover of the strength in Union". That made the turning point "as clear as daylight" (Ch III). When the secondary leader Atchison starts to rise, "the Big Six is about done and distribution is taking place" (Ch III).
15. **Chart role (figure charts).** Figure charts are "the best mechanical means of detecting accumulation and distribution". Raised bottoms after a long line warn of an upside push; lowering tops mean pressure has resumed. "The chart gives the direction of coming moves; the tape says 'when'" (Ch VIII). A long sideways zig-zag "accompanied by very small volume means lifelessness, but with normal or abnormally large volume, accumulation or distribution is more or less evidenced" (Ch VIII).
16. **Market-trend filter for swing trades.** A trader after 5–10-point moves "will make no commitments not in accordance with the trend" of a 10-leader composite, because a bull campaign "will not usually be pushed to completion in the face of a down trend in the general market" (Ch VIII).

### 3b. Exits and stops

- **Initial stop.** The "consensus … two points maximum gross loss on any one trade" is "purely arbitrary" (Ch IV). He prefers stops the market itself defines: ⅛ beyond the last "point of resistance". In his chart example he buys at 128¼ in a 128–129 range, after a third, slightly higher low, with the stop at 127⅞ (Ch IV).
- **Move to breakeven, then trail behind swing lows.** Once the stock clears the prior top, raise the stop to cost plus commission, then trail it under each new reaction low. An alternative is a mechanical trail 1 point behind the extreme until commissions are covered (Ch IV).
- **Never widen risk.** "Each trade is made on its own basis … should not be changed, except on the side of profit". Averaging: "Averaging is groping for the top or bottom. The Tape Reader must not grope. He must see and know, or he should not act" (Ch IV).
- **The four exits:** "(1) when the tape tells him to close; (2) when his stop is caught; (3) when his position is not clear; (4) when he has a large or satisfactory profit" (Ch IV). Often "when it is time to close a trade, it is usually time to reverse his position" (Ch IV).
- **No fixed profit target.** "If the strength increases with the advance it may run ten points" (Ch IV). In the late chapters he prefers closing at the culmination of each half-hour swing, especially on a "spectacular bid which looked like bait for outside buyers" (Ch XII).
- **Reaction vs reversal.** A 3-point rise followed by a 1–1½-point reversal "on light volume" is "a perfectly natural reaction and not a change of trend" (Ch X).

### 3c. When NOT to trade / no signal / market unreadable (emphasized by the author)

1. **Trendless, narrow market.** "A market which swings within a radius of a couple of points cannot be said to have a trend, and is a good one for the Tape Reader to avoid". The reason: costs make each trade "nearly half a point", so "each risk should contain a probable two to five points profit, or it is not justified" (Ch IV).
2. **Rudderless drift.** "When prices drift up and down without trend, like a ship without a rudder … the percentage of losing trades is apt to be high. When this condition continues it is well to hold off until the character of the market changes" (Ch XI).
3. **No positively indicated trend yet.** "The original commitment should, of course, be made only when the trend is positively indicated" (Ch IV). His Dec 21 1908 first loss came from buying on apparent inside support before any trend had formed: "When a stock holds steady within a half point radius it does not signify a reversal of trend, but rather a halting place" (Ch IX). He would not go short before "the thrust": "the Tape Reader cannot go with the trend until he is sure of a big swing" (Ch IV).
4. **Unclear position → exit or tighten, don't add.** "Uncertainty may be reduced by the use of stops … or by closing a trade whenever one's course is not entirely clear" (Ch IV). In the Jan 4 1909 example he exited a long because the undertone was weak, but did not go short until a definite cue appeared (Ch IV).
5. **During accumulation, don't buy yet.** "He does not buy it at once, because it may take weeks or months for the manipulator to complete his line". He waits for the mark-up to begin (Ch V).
6. **Don't short after a violent break.** He would be "wary about making an initial short sale of Smelters after a 15-point break, even if his indications were clear … the safest money was on the long side" (Ch VI). Also: "It is seldom that the market runs more than three or four consecutive days in one direction without a reaction, hence … his chances decrease as the swing is prolonged" (Ch VI).
7. **Volume signals can mislead.** "These volume indications are not always clear. Nor are they infallible" (Ch V). If one stock's volume swells far beyond the rest of the market's "with poor response from the rest of the list", suspect manipulation and be cautious (Ch V). A sudden burst of volume inside a range starts a move, "but not necessarily in the direction which is first indicated". Wait until the stock "breaks through its former limits with large and increasing volumes" (Ch X).
8. **Dull-market scalping:** "We cannot recommend it" (Ch VII). "We are not compelled to trade and results do not depend on how often we trade" (Ch VII). "It is not necessary for him to be in the market continuously" (Ch IV).
9. **Over-trading and too many stocks.** Trade one stock (Ch II). Take "only the very choicest" opportunities; "It is better to look on part of the time" (Ch IX, XI). Don't scalp both sides at once (Ch X).
10. **Personal state.** "If there be an unusual series of losses, the trader had better suspend operations until he discovers the cause" (Ch XI). Also stop for the day after poor sleep or poor health (Ch XI).
11. **Gauging extent is impossible early.** "It is impracticable and almost impossible to gauge the extent of a movement by its initial fluctuations" (Ch X).

## 4. Testable hypotheses on DAILY OHLCV (my operationalizations)

Defaults: liquid US stocks; `ATR20`; `V50` = 50-day average volume; forward returns measured from the next day's open, net of about 10 bps per side. Each signal is compared with matched unconditional baselines (same stock, same month).

- **H-WYC-1 — Dull-range breakout ("shakes itself loose from a narrow radius").** Setup: the 20-day high–low range divided by the close is in the bottom 20% of that stock's trailing 250-day distribution, and 20-day average volume is ≤ 0.8×V50. Trigger: close > prior 20-day high (long), or close < prior 20-day low (short), with breakout-day volume ≥ 1.5×V50. Measure: 5/10/20-day forward return in the breakout direction, and the "false-start rate" (close back inside the range within 3 days). Compare against breakouts with no prior contraction and breakouts with no volume confirmation. Variant: also require the market index to be above its 50-day MA (Ch VIII trend filter).
- **H-WYC-2 — Effort without result near highs (distribution).** Condition: stock up ≥ 15% over 40 days, close within 3% of its 60-day high, and a day with volume ≥ 2.5×V50 but true range ≤ 0.75×ATR20 and |close−prev close| ≤ 0.3×ATR20. Also tag clusters (≥ 2 such days within 10 days). Expectation: 10/20-day forward returns below baseline, and a higher probability of a ≥ 1-ATR drawdown. Mirror test near lows = absorption/accumulation (bullish).
- **H-WYC-3 — Climax after a protracted move.** Condition: 40-day return ≤ −20% (or ≥ +25%), and a day with true range ≥ 2.5×ATR20 and volume ≥ 3×V50 in the trend's direction. Expectation: mean reversion over 5–20 days. Also test the corollary that shorts started after such a decline do worse than shorts started earlier in the move (the "15-point break" warning).
- **H-WYC-4 — Rally-retracement rule.** Use a zig-zag with a 2×ATR20 threshold to identify swings. After a down-swing of size D, measure the first counter-rally R as a fraction of D. Buckets: < 0.33, 0.33–0.5, 0.5–0.67, > 0.67. Expectation: P(new swing low within 20 days) is highest for R < 0.5 ("the rally will fall short"), and in uptrends (close > 200-day MA) a recovery of ≥ 0.5 of a pullback predicts new highs.
- **H-WYC-5 — Up-volume vs down-volume in trends.** In an uptrend (close > 50-day MA, 50-day MA rising), compute ratio = mean volume on up-close days ÷ mean volume on down-close days over 20 days. Expectation: the top quintile of the ratio outperforms the bottom quintile over the next 20 days ("reactions must show a smaller volume than the advances"). A ratio < 0.8 inside an uptrend flags weakness.
- **H-WYC-6 — Relative resilience on market-shock days.** On index days ≤ −2% (or a 2σ down day), rank stocks by return minus beta-adjusted index return. Expectation: the top decile ("declined only ¼") outperforms the bottom decile over the next 20 days. Variant: a stock with its own ≥ 1-ATR gap down that closes above the prior close ("fail to break on bad news") → positive drift.
- **H-WYC-7 — Distribution under cover of the leaders (breadth divergence).** Condition: the cap-weighted index makes a 20-day high while the % of constituents above their 20-day MA is lower than at the prior 20-day high, or while the equal-weight index fails to confirm. Expectation: index 10/20-day forward returns below baseline.
- **H-WYC-8 — "Cannot be advanced → will decline."** Condition: ≥ 3 tests of a 20-day high within 1% over 10 days, each failing to close above it, on rising volume. Expectation: negative 10-day forward drift. If instead the level breaks on a close > high + 0.5×ATR, expect positive drift (dam-break logic, H-WYC-1).
- **H-WYC-9 — No-trade regime: trendless chop.** Define chop as Kaufman efficiency ratio(20) < 0.2, or 20-day range < 3×ATR20. Run generic trend entries (20-day breakout, 20/50 MA cross) and split results by regime. Expectation: expectancy after costs is ≤ 0 in chop and positive in trending regimes. Cost filter (Ch IV, "two to five points" vs a half-point round trip): skip stocks where ATR20/price < 4–10× the round-trip cost.
- **H-WYC-10 — Streak exhaustion.** After 4+ consecutive closes in one direction, measure P(next close in the same direction) and the 3/5-day forward return against the unconditional rate ("seldom … more than three or four consecutive days").
- **H-WYC-11 — Selling exhaustion / low-volume retest (my interpretation of Ch IX and the Ch VII shakeouts).** Condition: after a ≥ 15% decline, price makes a new 20-day low (or comes within 1% of the prior swing low) on volume ≤ 0.7× the volume at the prior low, and closes in the upper half of its range. Expectation: positive 10/20-day forward returns. Variant: an intraday undercut of a ≥ 20-day range low that closes back inside the range ("shake-out").
- **H-WYC-12 — Small giveback = strong hands.** Cross-sectional test: when the index has fallen ≥ 10% from its high, rank stocks by drawdown ÷ (prior 12-month low-to-high advance). Expectation: the lowest-giveback quintile leads in the next 60 trading days (UP 14.7% vs Steel 44.6%).

## 5. Caveats the author admits

- The rules are tentative and reasoned out as he went: "these recommendations and suggestions are not to be considered final or inflexible" (Ch IV). He revises himself openly. In Ch X he shifts from taking only wide swings to catching every important swing, and in Ch XII to taking half-hour swings.
- No mechanical formula exists. It "would be impossible to formulate a simple set of rules to fit every case or even the majority of them" (Ch XII).
- Volume indications "are not always clear. Nor are they infallible" (Ch V). Wash sales can fake volume (Ch IV, X).
- Charts: "I have yet to meet the man who has made money trading on a Figure Chart over an extended period". He proposes a blind, walk-forward test with fixed rules: hide the stock's name and slide paper across the chart (Ch VIII). "Any kind of a chart will show some profits at times". The figure-chart "count" for price targets has "very many exceptions" (Ch VIII).
- "Imaginary trades prove nothing in Tape Reading" (Ch V). Paper results flatter the trader (Ch II).
- He says his tape examples were read live, "at which time I did not know what was going to happen" (Ch VII). They are still selected examples.
- The hourly cycle (about 1 hour from top to top) is anecdotal: "Why this is I do not attempt to explain" (Ch VI). It cannot be tested on daily bars.
- Success is rare and needs full-time attention, risk capital and temperament. Costs (about 3/8 point per round trip) turn many small edges negative (Ch II, IV). Fast, public-dominated markets cause slippage (Ch X).

## 6. Verdict for the rulebook author (10 lines)

1. Its value is as a source of *conditions*, not formulas. Wyckoff says outright that tape reading cannot be reduced to rules.
2. The clearest no-trade rule: skip trendless, narrow, drifting markets unless they break out of the range with volume.
3. The core long setup is a contraction (dull range, drying volume) followed by an expansion breakout on volume, taken with the market trend.
4. The core warning is heavy volume with little price progress near highs (effort without result), especially when the rest of the list does not follow.
5. Relative strength *under pressure* (a stock that does not break on bad news or market shocks) is his most robust and most testable idea.
6. Use retracement arithmetic (½–⅔ normal; a rally short of ½ means the decline isn't over) as a regime and exit filter.
7. Exits: initial stop just beyond the last point of resistance, breakeven after the prior top is cleared, trail under swing lows, never average, never widen.
8. Daily-bar tests are proxies for intraday reasoning. Expect weaker effects and keep costs in every test (his own math shows costs dominate).
9. Run H-WYC-1, 2, 6, 9 and 12 first. They map most directly onto OHLCV and onto his strongest claims.
10. Treat his examples (1907–09, a manipulated market) as hypotheses that must be checked out-of-sample, which is exactly what he asked his readers to do.
