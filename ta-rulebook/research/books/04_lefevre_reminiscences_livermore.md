# 04 — Lefèvre, *Reminiscences of a Stock Operator* (1923): Livermore's trading rules

## 1. Bibliographic info and coverage

- **Book:** Edwin Lefèvre, *Reminiscences of a Stock Operator*. © 1923, George H. Doran Co. The Gutenberg scan is an American Research Council reprint. Its title page lists a Benton W. Davis introduction, but **that introduction is not in the Gutenberg text.**
- **Narrator:** "Larry Livingston," a stand-in for Jesse L. Livermore (the book is dedicated to him). Everything below is the narrator's claims, not audited results.
- **Source:** Project Gutenberg #60979 (plain text), downloaded 2026-10-31.
- **Read in full:** all 24 chapters (I–XXIV), text lines 113–10727, about **108,800 words** of body text (about 112k words with the Gutenberg boilerplate).
- **Terminology check:** **"pivotal point" never appears in this book.** That term is from Livermore's *How to Trade in Stocks* (1940), which I did not read. The nearest ideas here are "resistance points," a "movement-limit," and first crosses of 100/200/300. **"Volume" appears only 6 times, and never as a rule.** Livermore reads *how stock is absorbed* (the price response to buying and selling). Any volume rule below is **my proxy, marked [interp]**.
- **Conversions:** 1 point ≈ 1% on the ~$100 stocks of his day. I convert points to % or ATR **[interp]**.

## 2. Core beliefs (chapter references in brackets)

- **The tape tells you what, not why.** "Your business with the tape is now--not to-morrow. The reason can wait" [I]. The warning comes before the break, and the reason comes after [XVII].
- **Repetition comes from unchanging human nature.** "Whatever happens in the stock market to-day has happened before and will happen again" [I].
- **The line of least resistance.** "prices, like everything else, move along the line of least resistance" [X]. Once a move "fairly starts," its direction is evident [X].
- **Only the right side, never a favourite side.** "only one side to the stock market... not the bull side or the bear side, but the right side" [III]. "In a bear market all stocks go down and in a bull market they go up" [VII].
- **The big money is in the big swing and comes from sitting tight.** "It never was my thinking that made the big money for me. It was always my sitting" [V]. "Men who can both be right and sit tight are uncommon" [V].
- **Price level is irrelevant to timing.** "The price, per se, has nothing to do with establishing my line of least resistance" [X].
- **Timing is a second problem, separate from direction.** "My position was right but my play was wrong" [VIII]. "The way to make big money is to be right at exactly the right time" [IX].
- **The trend comes before the news.** "The trend has been established before the news is published, and in bull markets bear items are ignored and bull news exaggerated, and vice versa" [X].
- **Two allies.** "the man who is right always has two forces working in his favor--basic conditions and the men who are wrong" [X].
- **The enemies are internal.** Hope and fear: "Instead of hoping he must fear; instead of fearing he must hope" [X].
- **Charts and systems help but can mislead.** The average chart reader becomes "obsessed with the notion that the dips and peaks and primary and secondary movements are all there is" [V]. A chart system won until WWI "knocked all precedents into a cocked hat" [V].
- **Humility.** "no man living can beat the stock market!" You can only beat individual deals [X, XXIV].

## 3. Explicit decision rules

### 3a. When to act

- **Regime first.** Tape timing assumes "I have checked up general conditions pretty well and they are bullish" [VII]. "be bullish in a bull market and bearish in a bear market" [VIII]. In 1907, tightening money (call-money spikes, railroads racing for capital) set the bear call [VIII–IX].
- **Range breakout.** In "between-swings times" prices fluctuate in a range (his example: 120–130). The trader "should wait until the tape tells him that the time is ripe" [X]. The signal is a break of the "movement-limit," fuelled by the trapped side covering [X]. Wheat traded "for months" between $1.10 and $1.20. "it closed at above $1.19," and he bought the next open at $1.20½ [X].
- **First crosses and new records.** "whenever a stock crosses 100 or 200 or 300 for the first time, it nearly always keeps going up for 30 to 50 points" [XIV]. "Timid people don't like to buy a stock at a new high record" [IX]. Bethlehem in 1915: he waited six weeks for par, then bought at 98–99 when the tape "used a megaphone" [XIV]. When Union Pacific made new high records, he said: "This is no stock for me to be short of" [VI].
- **Buy on a rising scale, sell on a falling one.** "I don't buy long stock on a scale down, I buy on a scale up." "each sale must be at a lower level than the previous sale" [VII]. "I like to pay top prices" [X].
- **Absorption tells you who is right.** "The more I bought the stronger it got, without any spurt" [VI]. Deacon White sold into rumoured buying. The stock "was absorbed by a market that did not stop rising," so he bought [VII].
- **Bear entries.** "Never try to sell at the top. It isn't wise. Sell after a reaction if there is no rally" [XIV]. Sell "when the rally began to falter and pause" [VIII].
- **Divergence from the market, both ways.** A stock marked up "in the face of the heaviness of the general list" was a short [XVIII]. A stock taking support while the market declined was a long. That was "a trading condition in a certain stock and not... a speculative theory in general" [XVIII].

### 3b. Sizing and pyramiding

- **Rule.** "after the initial transaction, don't make a second unless the first shows you a profit. Wait and watch" [VII]. "Let him buy one-fifth of his full line. If that does not show him a profit he must not increase his holdings" [X].
- **Cotton ladder [X].** Buy 10k bales. Add 10k at +10 points. Add 20k at +20 points. If the first lots show a loss, "out I'd go." The probe losses were the price of having "a line of cotton in every real movement."
- **Stock ladder [VII].** Three 2,000-share lots at 110→114, then stop adding. On the rally after a reaction, bid for 4,000. If they fill *easily*, "something is wrong" and he sends a testing sale. If they fill on a scale up, "Then I know I am right."
- **Why.** "have the big bet down only when you win, and when you lose to lose only a small exploratory bet" [X].
- **Limits.** He does not advise "persistent pyramiding" [VII]. Late in a move he had "no intention of increasing my line" [XVII].
- **Pat Hearne's variant [X].** Add a unit per 1% rise, with a stop one point under the last buy, trailed up.
- **Never average down.** "Of all speculative blunders there are few greater than trying to average a losing game... Always sell what shows you a loss and keep what shows you a profit" [XII].
- **Size to stay calm.** "Sell down to the sleeping point" [X].

### 3c. Exits and stops

- **Failed follow-through.** Anaconda was bought on its 300 cross. "On a legitimate advance the price should have gone to 310 without stopping." When it fell back to 301 he sold everything at market [IX]. "the only thing to do when a man is wrong is to be right by ceasing to be wrong" [IX].
- **Speed.** "When you want to get out, get out" [IX]. No limit orders [III]. "to feel that I am wrong and to decide to get out are practically one process" [XVII].
- **Character, not a number.** "It is the character of the advance--or of the decline--that determines for me the correctness or the fallacy of my market position" [XVIII]. Ch XVII cotton: sell-offs kept being followed by "a new high for the move," so the line was up. When a 50-point drop "had not rallied as it had been in the habit of doing," the line had turned, and he went short.
- **End of an uptrend.** When, "after a long steady rise a stock turns and gradually begins to go down, with only occasional small rallies," then "the line of least resistance has changed from upward to downward" [XXIV].
- **Fallen leaders.** Former leaders "reacted several points from the top and--for the first time in many months--did not come back." They "dropped six or seven points and stayed there" while new leaders advanced [XIV]. He sold them.
- **Weak group.** He sold a stock in a bull market when "other stocks in the same group were not acting bullishly" [XVII].
- **Absorption and climax.** Sell into "a great big market with a tremendous power of absorption," which "usually comes at the end of the move": 72k U.S. Steel sold one point off the top [XVII]. "in a bear market it is always wise to cover if complete demoralisation suddenly develops" [XIV].
- **Hold through noise.** He let counter-trend rallies eat half his paper profit rather than lose his position [V, XVIII]. "Nobody can catch all the fluctuations" [V].

### 3d. When NOT to act

- **Narrow range.** "there is no sense in trying to anticipate what the next big movement is going to be--up or down." Wait "until the price breaks through the limit in either direction" [X]. Trying to push cotton out of its range cost him $200k [X].
- **Overtrading.** "the Wall Street fool, who thinks he must trade all the time" [II]. "The desire for constant action irrespective of underlying conditions is responsible for many losses" [II]. "I traded in and out of season, every day, whether or not conditions were right" [VIII].
- **Wrong behaviour.** "If a stock doesn't act right don't touch it... No diagnosis, no prognosis. No prognosis, no profit" [V]. "I never buy a stock even in a bull market, if it doesn't act as it ought to act in that kind of market" [XVII].
- **Moneyless or dull markets.** In 1911–14, trading anyway was "trying to force the market into giving me what it didn't have to give" [XIV]. He stood aside in dull summers [IX] and after a decline "had discounted the immediate future" [VIII]. Dead stocks are corpses you "lug" [XXI].
- **Cheap or dear.** "millions upon millions of dollars have been lost by men who bought stocks because they looked cheap or sold them because they looked dear" [X]. The semisucker "measures his bargains by the number of points it has sold off from the top" [V]. Post-peak bargain hunters are who distributors sell to on the way down [XX, XXII].
- **Tips and deference.** "If I buy stocks on Smith's tip I must sell those same stocks on Smith's tip" [III]. His worst losses came from deferring to Harding [VI], Percy Thomas [XII] and Williamson [XIII].
- **Fighting the group.** "it is not wise to buck against what I may call the manifest group-tendency." "beware of buying a stock that refuses to follow the group-leader" [XVII].
- **Sprinting ahead of the signal.** "it is well not to begin selling in bulk until there is no danger of the engine back-firing"; he "should have walked and not sprinted" [VIII]. Coffee was right but early, and lost money [XV].
- **Bad mental state.** "sick, nervous, upset and unable to reason calmly" is the state in which no speculator should trade [XIII]. Never make the market pay for a bill [XII].
- **"Too high" shorts.** Cornered shorts sold because a stock "never before had sold so high" [XIX]. Cammack: "Don't sell stocks when the sap is running up the trees!" [XI].

## 4. Testable hypotheses on daily OHLCV

Conventions **[interp]**:
- Signal at the close, enter at the next open, 10 bp/side cost.
- `MKT_UP` = index close above a rising 200-day SMA. "Group" = GICS industry equal-weight index.
- Always compare against the unconditional baseline over the same universe and dates.
- Maps onto `tagym` (`hi/lo/chi`, `volr`, `mkt_up`, `rs`, `swings`, `simulate_trades`).

**H-LIV-1 — Range breakout.**
- *Setup:* prior 60 days (sensitivity 40/120) with `(maxH−minL)/minL ≤ 15%`. Wheat ≈ 9%; the 120–130 example ≈ 8%.
- *Long:* close > prior 60-day high. *Short:* close < prior 60-day low.
- *Filter:* side agrees with `MKT_UP`.
- *Predictions:* 20/60-day excess return > 0. In-range entries near the range low (bought because they "looked cheap") do worse than breakouts. Regime-aligned breakouts beat counter-regime ones.

**H-LIV-2 — First cross of 100/200/300.**
- *Data:* **unadjusted** closes.
- *Event:* close ≥ L ∈ {100, 200, 300} while all prior closes in the data are < L.
- *Claim:* +30 to +50 points "nearly always," faster above 300.
- *Test:* P(+30 points before a close < L−3%) and days-to-target, against matched all-time-high events at non-round prices.
- *Prediction:* round levels beat matched non-round levels.

**H-LIV-3 — New highs vs. "bargains."**
- *In `MKT_UP`, compare:* (a) close at a 252-day high; (b) close 15–25% below its 252-day high but above the 200-day SMA.
- *Prediction:* (a) beats (b) on 20/60/120-day excess return. Falsified if (b) ≥ (a) net.

**H-LIV-4 — Probe-and-pyramid vs. full size.**
- *Signals:* H-LIV-1/3 entries.
- *Variant P:* 20% at entry, +20% at close ≥ entry+1 ATR, +60% at ≥ entry+2 ATR. Exit all if the probe closes ≤ entry−1 ATR first.
- *Variant F:* 100% at entry, same stop.
- *Common exit:* close < 50-day low or the H-LIV-9 flip.
- *Prediction:* P has better return per unit of capital at risk, a thinner left tail and lower max drawdown.
- *Sub-test (Hearne):* +1 unit per +1 ATR, stop 1 ATR below the last add.

**H-LIV-5 — Failed follow-through exit.**
- *Breakout set:* H-LIV-1/2 breakouts.
- *Failure flag:* within 3 days the high never reaches breakout + max(3%, 1 ATR), **and** a close is ≤ breakout + 0.3%.
- *Prediction:* flagged breakouts have negative 20-day excess return. Exiting on the flag beats a fixed 2-ATR stop.

**H-LIV-6 — Averaging down.**
- *Variant A:* add a unit at each −1 ATR close, up to 3 adds.
- *Compare:* against H-LIV-4 P on the same signals.
- *Prediction:* A has lower expectancy per unit of capital and a fatter worst-1% loss.

**H-LIV-7 — Fallen leaders in a rising market.**
- *Leaders:* top decile by 126-day relative strength.
- *Event:* a leader closes ≥ 6% below its 60-day high, makes no new 60-day high in the next 20 days, and the index is within 3% of its 252-day high.
- *Predictions:* the stock's 40/60-day excess return < 0. When > 40% of leaders are in this state, index 60–120-day returns fall below the mean (the "cease to be a bull market long before prices generally begin to break" warning).
- *Short add:* only once each short shows ≥ 4% profit and the index closes below its 50-day SMA (his "doubled up" rule).

**H-LIV-8 — Group confirmation and the laggard trap.**
- *(a) Filter:* take longs only if the group index is above its 50-day and 200-day SMAs. Prediction: beats unfiltered.
- *(b) "Chester" event:* the group is in the top tercile by 63-day return while the stock's 63-day return is ≤ 0 and it makes a 20-day low. Prediction: 60-day excess < 0. Buying it as a catch-up play ("every dog has his day") loses.

**H-LIV-9 — Line-of-least-resistance flip.**
- *Context:* after a 126-day return ≥ +25% above a rising 200-day SMA.
- *Down-flip* when both hold:
  - a confirmed lower swing high *and* lower swing low (fractal k=5);
  - a down day ≥ 1.5 ATR with no close above that day's open within 5 sessions. Before the flip, such days were "bought" quickly.
- *Up-flip:* the mirror image.
- *Prediction:* the down-flip has negative 20/60-day excess. As a long exit it beats a fixed % trailing stop on return per unit of drawdown.

**H-LIV-10 — Short after a failed rally, not at the top.**
- *Setup:* the stock is ≥ 15% off its 252-day high and below a falling 50-day SMA. It rallies ≥ 1.5 ATR off a 20-day low but stalls below the prior swing high.
- *Entry:* a close below the rally's starting low.
- *Compare with:* shorting the first close below the 50-day SMA near the high.
- *Prediction:* the failed-rally entry has a better win rate and smaller maximum adverse excursion.

**H-LIV-11 — Climax exits** **[interp: volume thresholds are mine]**.
- *Shorts:* in a downtrend, cover on a gap-down ≥ 2 ATR with volume ≥ 3× the 50-day average.
- *Longs:* after a ≥ 50% 126-day advance, sell into a ≥ 3× volume day that closes in the top quartile of its range.
- *Prediction:* 10–20-day returns after these days oppose the prior trend.

**H-LIV-12 — No-trade regimes and overtrading.**
- *(a) Chop:* suppress entries while the 60-day range ≤ 15% and ADX < 20, unless H-LIV-1 fires.
- *(b) Dull market:* suppress entries when |index 126-day return| < 5% and realized vol is in its bottom tercile.
- *(c) Frequency:* trade every signal vs. only signals with regime + group + breakout aligned.
- *Prediction:* (a) and (b) raise net expectancy, and the selective book in (c) wins net of costs.

## 5. Caveats: what the book says fails

- **Tape reading alone failed** under lag, slippage and his own market impact: "tape reading is not enough" [III]. Model next-open fills, and distrust thin stocks, where one market order moved the print [IV].
- **Early = wrong in P&L.** He went broke being correctly bearish in 1906 [VIII]. Coffee was right but early [XV].
- **Regime breaks** destroyed a precedent-based chart system [V]. Validate across sub-periods.
- **The "unexpectable"** (the Lusitania, the 1917 note, price-fixing, rule changes) sits outside any rule [XIV–XV]. Size for gaps.
- **Hunches** are re-explained as accumulated small warnings [VI, XVII]. They are not codifiable.
- **Internal contradiction.** Against Reading he let "conditions" override a tape saying "Leave it alone!" [VIII]. With Tropical Trading, the tape overrode his bearish view [XVIII]. Let data decide which dominates in each regime.
- **Anecdotes, not statistics.** The episodes are selected and dramatized, and the narrator went broke repeatedly.
- **Manufactured activity.** Chapters XIX–XXII show volume and activity can be engineered for distribution. A volume spike in a dead stock late in a cycle (Consolidated Stove) was a *sell*.
- **Round numbers (H-LIV-2)** may not survive splits or modern price scales.
- **Shorts** need squeeze controls. He capped a short at a share of the floating supply [XVIII].

## 6. Verdict for the rulebook author (10 lines)

1. The core lesson is **when not to trade**: no trend, no confirmation, a dull market, a stock or group acting wrong, or a tip.
2. The signal is a **break of a defined range or prior record**, aligned with the general market and the stock's group.
3. Price level is irrelevant. New highs are buys in bull markets; "bargains" off the highs are traps (H-LIV-3).
4. Sizing is probe-then-pyramid on open profit, and never averaging down. This is the most testable and most robust idea (H-LIV-4/6).
5. Exits are behavioural: failed follow-through, sell-offs that stop recovering, leaders that don't come back. No fixed targets.
6. Regime comes first: long in bull markets, short in bear markets, flat in "moneyless" markets.
7. Short after a failed rally, adding as it goes lower. Never short "too high" stocks in a rising market.
8. Volume is nearly absent from the text. Label any volume rule as the author's proxy.
9. "Pivotal points" are not in this book. Cite *How to Trade in Stocks* (1940) for that term.
10. Prioritize H-LIV-1, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8, 9 and 12. Treat H-LIV-2 and 11 as folklore to falsify.
