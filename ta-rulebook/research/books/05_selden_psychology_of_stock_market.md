# 05 — Selden, *Psychology of the Stock Market* (1912): sentiment, crowds, and when a signal is real

## 1. Bibliographic info and coverage

- **Book:** G. C. Selden, *Psychology of the Stock Market*. Ticker Publishing Company, New York, 1912; the preface is dated May 28, 1912. Selden describes himself as a former Columbia fellow, a news writer, a statistician, and an editor at *The Magazine of Wall Street*.
- **Source:** Project Gutenberg #75570 (plain text, released 2025-03-09), downloaded 2026-10-31.
- **Read in full:** the Preface and all eight chapters:
  - I The Speculative Cycle
  - II Inverted Reasoning and Its Consequences
  - III "They"
  - IV Confusing the Present with the Future—Discounting
  - V Confusing the Personal with the General
  - VI The Panic and the Boom
  - VII The Psychology of Scale Orders
  - VIII The Mental Attitude of the Individual
- **Length:** about **17,600 words** of author text (about 21.3k with boilerplate and the publisher's back-page ads).
- **Note:** the back-matter ads include the line "It is better to know which way the general market is likely to swing than to know earnings." That is **Ticker Publishing advertising, not Selden.** Do not cite it as his view.
- **Character of the text:** qualitative essays. There are very few numbers: "five or ten dollars a share" minor swings, 6%/4% investor yield limits, a "half point down for three points" scale, and distribution lasting "a month or more." Every threshold below is **my interpretation, marked [interp]**.

## 2. Core beliefs

- **Two layers of price movement.** "minor fluctuations, amounting to perhaps five or ten dollars a share in the active speculative issues, are chiefly psychological." "The broad movements of the market, covering periods of months or even years, are always the result of general financial conditions" [I].
- **The speculative cycle repeats at every scale:**
  - dull, unnoticed rise;
  - shorts cover;
  - the public waits for reactions that "do not appear";
  - then the public chases;
  - distribution while "the general average of prices is now rising very slowly, if at all";
  - "the decline is usually more rapid than the previous advance";
  - a "bargain day" when the load "strikes an area honeycombed with stop loss orders";
  - a quick rally, a sag, and lethargy again.

  The cycle is "substantially uniform, whether the cycle be one covering a week, a month, or a year" [I].
- **Opinion is weighted by money.** It is "measured in dollars, not in population." So "the great body of opinion appears to be bullish at the top and bearish at the bottom," and those who write and talk "are more likely to be wrong than right" [II].
- **Contrarian reasoning works only at extremes.** "A market which repeatedly refuses to respond to good news after a considerable advance is likely to be 'full of stocks.'" One that "will not go down on bad news is usually 'bare of stocks'" [II]. In the middle of a trend, "It is during a long upward movement that the 'lamb' makes money, because he accepts facts as facts," while cynical professionals fight the advance [II].
- **Discounting.** "the logical moment for the highest prices is when the news is most bullish" [IV]. "an item of news usually causes but one considerable movement of prices" [II]. "Extreme prices will come at the time when the news is most emphatic and most widely disseminated" [IV].
- **Your own position bends your judgment.** This is "the greatest obstacle to success" [II]. "In the market, to be consistent is to be stubborn" [V].
- **Panics come from necessity, booms from irresponsibility.** "The panic is not a sudden development." "The actual bottom prices of the panic are more likely to result from necessity than from fear" [VI]. Booms are carried by shoestring-margin traders. Sound shorts "are right, but right too soon" [VI].
- **Support and pressure come from scale orders.** Patient ("phlegmatic") capital buys on a scale down and sells on a scale up. It absorbs reactions in bull markets and caps rallies in bear markets. When it withdraws, the trend ends [VII].
- **He distrusts mechanical rules.** "the market student of a mathematical turn of mind is always seeking a rule or a set of rules—a 'sure thing'" [III]. "Historical parallels are likely to be misleading. Every situation is new, though usually composed of familiar elements" [IV].
- **Temperament.** "In the market you are nothing but a chip on the tide of events." "Any emotion—enthusiasm, fear, anger, depression—will only cloud the intellect" [VIII].

## 3. Explicit decision rules

### 3a. When to act

- **Go with the tide.** For the active trader, "his prime object is to 'go with the tide.'" "Endeavor to catch the trend of sentiment. Even if this should be temporarily against fundamental conditions, it is nevertheless unprofitable to oppose it" [VIII].
- **Use contrarian reasoning only at extremes.** A market that won't rise on good news after a big advance is distributing; one that won't fall on bad news is accumulating [II].
  - "Be suspicious of bull news at high prices, and of bear news at low prices" [II].
  - "be ready to sell at the moment when bull sentiment seems to be most widely distributed, and to buy when the public in general seem most discouraged" [V].
  - "When the market looks weakest, when the news is at the worst, when bearish prognostications are most general, is the time to buy" [III].
- **Buy at the most urgent point, not merely a low price.** In 1907 the best entry was the Monday after the bank statement with the largest drop in reserves; prices "never sold so low afterward." In 1900, the low came on Bryan's nomination, "the worst that could happen" [IV].
- **Wait for support before buying a bear-market low.** "low prices, after a prolonged bear period, are not in themselves a sufficient reason for buying stocks." Many buyers find "it was a false bottom" [VI]. The end of a bear market is "the reappearance of 'support' and the removal of 'pressure,' so that prices rebound quickly and sharply from declines" [VII].
- **Watch how reactions resolve in a bull market.** A reaction lasts until the scale orders underneath absorb the floating supply. Then "far less resistance will be encountered on the advance than was met on the reaction; hence prices rise to a new high level" [VII].
- **Early in a new advance, the first real reaction is buyable.** "the market looks 'too weak to buy,' and when it starts up again it often does so with a sudden leap" that leaves waiting buyers behind [I].
- **Scale in when you are prone to overconfidence.** "He makes only a part of his full commitment at the moment when he feels most confident, holding the remainder in reserve" [VIII].

### 3b. Exits

- **Decide from the market's position, not yours.** Forget "his own position in the market, his profits or losses, the relation of present prices to the point where he bought or sold." "If the market is going down the trader must sell, no matter whether he has a profit or a loss" [V].
- **Take profits when the move culminates,** "regardless of your own deal" [V]. Don't settle for a fixed five points if you think the market goes higher [V].
- **No arbitrary brackets.** On a fixed 7-point target and 2.5-point stop in Union Pacific: "Nothing could be more foolish than these arbitrary figures." "Stop your losses; let your profits run" is empty without knowing "where to stop the losses and how far to let the profits run" [V].
- **End-of-bull signs:**
  - "Prices go down easily and on larger transactions, while advances are sluggish and opposition is met at higher levels" [VII];
  - scale buyers withdraw and scale sellers appear, after which "The bull market will not go much further" [VII];
  - a top-area range "fluctuating within comparatively narrow limits for a month or more" (distribution) [VII];
  - repeated failure to rally on good news [II].
- **Money signals (not OHLCV).** The end of a boom is "exhaustion of liquid capital." It shows in higher call-money rates, shrinking bank excess deposits over loans, rising commercial-paper rates, and "a sagging market for high-grade bonds" [VI]. Modern analogues (my suggestion): short rates and IG bond prices.
- **The climax rule.** "refuse to follow the market beyond what you consider a reasonable climax, no matter how large the possible profits" [VIII].

### 3c. When NOT to act

- **Doubt.** "When in doubt, keep out of the market. Delays cost less than losses" [VIII].
- **Evenly balanced evidence.** When technical indications are evenly balanced, an uninvolved trader "would be likely to think it wise to stay out until he could see his way more clearly" [V]. Longs read the same picture as bullish and shorts as bearish.
- **Heavy, anxious, or hasty trading.** "do not act hastily on apparently sensational information; do not trade so heavily as to become anxious" [VIII].
- **Shorting a genuine bull market for overvaluation.** Those shorts "are nearly always driven to cover by a further rise," because "a bull market nearly always goes unreasonably high before it culminates" [V, VI].
- **Buying merely because prices are low** after a long bear market (false bottoms) [VI].
- **Trading on past events.** After strong earnings, the right thought is "Prices have risen to the extent represented by these increased earnings... Now what next?" [IV]. Overdiscounted news causes declines on announcement [IV].
- **Inverted reasoning used to defend a position.** "If you are long or short of the market, you are not an unprejudiced judge" [II]. Expect to see the signs you want: "there is no bear so bearish as a sold out bull who wants a chance to repurchase" [V].
- **Single-factor "notions."** "Beware of saying, 'This is the most important factor in the situation,' unless the action of the market shows that others agree with you" [VIII].
- **"They" stories.** "They will have to put them higher to make a market to sell on" is a late-bull rationalization that keeps buyers buying [III].
- **Emotion or stubbornness.** "Sometimes it may become necessary to close all commitments and remain out of the market for a few days" [VIII].
- **Trusting a big campaign in uncertain times.** "Great campaigns can only be undertaken with safety in periods when the future is to a certain extent assured" [III].

## 4. Testable hypotheses on daily OHLCV

Conventions as in file 04 **[interp]**: signal at the close, entry at the next open, 10 bp/side costs, an unconditional baseline, and `MKT_UP` = index above a rising 200-day SMA. Volume is in shares. "Index" = SPY (or the equal-weight universe) unless stated. H-SEL-3b needs an earnings calendar.

**H-SEL-1 — Support vs. pressure (up/down volume asymmetry) [VII].**
- *Measure:* `UDVR_n = Σ volume on up-close days / Σ volume on down-close days` over n=20 (sensitivity 10/50). `DAR_n` = mean |return| on down days ÷ mean return on up days.
- *Distribution flag:* after a 252-day gain ≥ +30% with the close within 5% of the 252-day high: `UDVR_20 < 0.8` and `DAR_20 > 1.2` ("go down easily and on larger transactions, while advances are sluggish").
- *Accumulation flag:* the mirror case, ≥ 25% below the 252-day high with `UDVR_20 > 1.25`.
- *Predictions:* the distribution flag gives negative 20/60-day excess return and the accumulation flag positive, at both stock and index level. Compare with OBV-slope and CMF baselines.

**H-SEL-2 — Top and bottom ranges ("a month or more") [I, VII].**
- *Top context:* 252-day gain ≥ +40% and price within 7% of the 252-day high.
- *Bottom context:* ≥ 30% below the 252-day high.
- *Range:* the 20-day high-low range ≤ 8% (or ≤ 3 ATR) for ≥ 20 sessions.
- *"Churn" add-on (top only):* 20-day average volume ≥ 1.2× the 100-day average while 20-day net change is < +2% (the average "rising very slowly, if at all").
- *Predictions:* top ranges with churn break down more often than up (versus the H-LIV-1 unconditional break-direction base rate), and bottom ranges break up more often. After a top-range breakdown, the decline is faster in %/day than the preceding advance ("more rapid than the previous advance").

**H-SEL-3 — "Full of stocks" / "bare of stocks" [II].**
- *(a) OHLCV-only proxy.* A *bad-news day* is an index return ≤ −2%.
  - A stock that closes ≥ 0 on ≥ 2 such days within 60 sessions is "bare of stocks." Prediction: positive 60-day excess.
  - A *good-news day* is an index return ≥ +1.5%. A stock with a ≥ +30% 126-day run that closes < 0 on ≥ 2 such days within 60 sessions is "full of stocks." Prediction: negative 60-day excess.
- *(b) With an earnings calendar.*
  - After a ≥ +30% 126-day run, a positive EPS surprise with a day 0–1 return < 0 is bearish; mirror it for misses after large declines.
  - Discounting sub-test ("but one considerable movement"): events in the top decile of [−20, −1]-day excess run-up should show post-event 20-day excess ≤ 0. Events without a run-up should drift with the surprise.

**H-SEL-4 — "Bargain day," false bottoms, and the support confirmation [I, IV, VI, VII].**
- *Bargain day:* the index is ≥ 20% below its 252-day high, ≥ 60 sessions after the peak, with a day of return ≤ −3% (or ≤ −2.5 ATR) on volume ≥ 2× the 50-day average.
- *Predictions:*
  - positive 5–10-day returns ("a quick rally follows");
  - a high probability of a lower low within 40 sessions ("prices sag again," "false bottom");
  - no reliable 60-day edge from buying the bargain day alone, nor from buying on drawdown depth alone (e.g. index −30%).
- *Support confirmation:* after the low, two separate down days ≥ 1.5 ATR, each fully recovered within 3 sessions ("rebound quickly and sharply"), and/or `UDVR_20 > 1.25`.
- *Prediction:* buying on confirmation gives better 60/120-day return per unit of drawdown than buying the bargain day.

**H-SEL-5 — "Right, but right too soon" [VI].**
- *Event:* close ≥ 25% above the 200-day SMA and RSI(14) ≥ 80.
- *Short tests:* (i) short at once; (ii) short only after the close falls below the 50-day SMA.
- *Split by regime:* `MKT_UP` plus rising breadth vs. otherwise.
- *Prediction:* (i) has negative expectancy in `MKT_UP` (the "riot of pyramided margins"). (ii) is better, and is the only version worth keeping.

**H-SEL-6 — First "too weak to buy" reaction of a new advance [I].**
- *Context:* the stock (or index) spent ≥ 120 sessions ≥ 20% below its 252-day high, then makes a new 252-day closing high.
- *Event:* the *first* subsequent pullback of ≥ 8% (or ≥ 3 ATR) from the post-breakout high that holds above the breakout level.
- *Entry:* the first close above the prior 5-day high after the pullback low.
- *Prediction:* positive 20/60-day excess, larger than for the 3rd+ pullback in the same advance. This reconciles Selden with Livermore's warning against late-cycle dip buying.

**H-SEL-7 — Market-based exits beat arbitrary brackets and breakeven-hoping [V].**
- *Entries:* identical, e.g. H-LIV-1 breakouts.
- *Exit F (fixed bracket):* +3 ATR target / −1 ATR stop (the 7 / 2.5-point UP example scaled).
- *Exit M (market state):* exit on an H-SEL-1 distribution flag, a close < 20-day low, or an index close below its 50-day SMA, regardless of P&L.
- *Exit B (breakeven-hoping):* hold losers until they return to entry or reach −15%.
- *Prediction:* M ≥ F > B on net expectancy and on return per unit of drawdown.

**H-SEL-8 — "When in doubt, keep out" [V, VIII].**
- *Clear state:* the index, the group index, and the stock (50-day and 200-day SMAs) all point the same way, and `UDVR_20` agrees.
- *Mixed state:* anything else.
- *Prediction:* identical entry signals have lower mean and higher variance in the mixed state. Skipping mixed-state trades raises Sharpe with small opportunity cost. Report the forgone return explicitly.

**H-SEL-9 — Peak strength marks late-cycle tops, conditioned on cycle age [V, VIII].** Selden: "the very moment when the market looks strongest, is likely to be near the top."
- *Event:* index 20-day return in the top 2% of its history *and* ≥ 90% of stocks above their 50-day SMA.
- *Split:* late cycle (≥ 18 months since the last 20% drawdown, index ≥ 60% above its cycle low) vs. early cycle.
- *Prediction:* late-cycle events give below-mean 60/120-day returns. Early-cycle events do not (the "lamb" makes money mid-trend). If both are bullish, Selden's claim fails on modern data.

**H-SEL-10 — Fractal uniformity [I].**
- *Test:* rerun H-SEL-1/2/4 with every lookback scaled ×0.25 and ×4 (weekly-like and quarterly-like cycles).
- *Prediction:* same sign of effect at all scales, which is Selden's "substantially uniform" claim. The magnitude may differ.

## 5. Caveats: what Selden says fails

- **Selden himself is against rule-books.** He advises "avoid all efforts to reduce the movement of prices to rules, measures, or similarities and to analyze each case by itself" [IV]. Encoding him is a deliberate liberty, so treat these hypotheses as checks on his claims, not as his system.
- **Contrarian reasoning cuts both ways.** It "permits of putting an alternate construction on any event." Taken too far it wrecks traders' thinking ("mental wrecks... chairwarmers") [II]. Use it only at extremes, with tape confirmation.
- **Simple reversal of the crowd won't work.** By 1912 the public's "wrongheadedness... no longer exists to the same extent," and readers "probably could not make profits by reversing" themselves [V].
- **Confirmation bias corrupts the signals.** A trader positioned short sees "holes" (sudden failures to absorb stock) that aren't there. The same goes for "distribution" after a rise [V]. Compute signals mechanically, independent of the book's positions.
- **Discounting is uneven.** Some events can't be discounted (earthquakes, court rulings). Others are overdiscounted, and insiders' foresight "is, however, usually overestimated" [IV].
- **Panic-bottom timing is hard.** Final lows come from forced liquidation, often below "low enough." Buying on low prices alone fails [VI].
- **Top-calling is harder still.** It is "more difficult to distinguish the end of a stock market boom than to decide when a panic is definitely over" [VI].
- **"They" theories** are mostly vague. Analyze the sources of buying and selling instead [III].
- **Dated institutions.** Call money, clearing-house deposits and floor-trader tactics need modern proxies. Volume conventions have changed (HFT, ETFs, options), which weakens H-SEL-1 and H-SEL-2 on recent data.

## 6. Verdict for the rulebook author (10 lines)

1. Selden explains *why* signals exist (crowd psychology, discounting, absorption of supply) more than he supplies triggers.
2. His main **no-signal rule**: in doubt, or with evenly balanced indications, stay out. "Delays cost less than losses" (H-SEL-8).
3. Trend-following is his default for active traders ("go with the tide"). Fading is valid only at sentiment extremes.
4. The most testable OHLCV idea: bull markets fall on light activity and rise on heavy, and tops reverse this (H-SEL-1/2).
5. A market that won't rally on good news is "full of stocks"; one that won't fall on bad news is "bare of stocks." This is a strong filter, but only at extremes. It needs a news proxy (H-SEL-3).
6. Low prices are not a buy signal. Wait for "support": declines rebounding quickly (H-SEL-4).
7. Don't short overvaluation in a genuine bull market. Shorts are "right too soon" (H-SEL-5).
8. Exits should be market-state based and ignore entry price. Fixed brackets are "foolish" (H-SEL-7).
9. He is explicitly anti-mechanical. Present his hypotheses as falsifiable claims, with sub-period checks.
10. Pair him with Livermore (file 04). Both say timing and confirmation matter as much as direction, and both say "when in doubt, keep out."
